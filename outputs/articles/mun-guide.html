Beyond the Classroom

A Beginner’s Guide to Model United Nations

Understanding the procedures, debates and responsibilities of a delegate.

For first-timers, read Tips for MUN First-Timers after becoming familiar with the general procedure.

What is Model United Nations?

Model United Nations, commonly known as MUN, is an academic simulation of the United Nations in which students take on the roles of delegates representing different countries. Through research, debate, negotiation and diplomacy, participants work together to address some of the world’s pressing political, social, economic and humanitarian issues.

MUN is also an opportunity to understand different perspectives, defend a country’s interests, find common ground and work towards solutions that benefit the international community. Each participant represents an assigned country within a specific committee. Delegates research and represent their country’s actual policies, political positions and interests rather than expressing personal opinions. This guide covers the fundamentals of MUN procedure, from preparation to debating, negotiating and voting on resolutions.

1. Understanding your role as a delegate

Before a conference, every delegate is assigned a country and a committee. The delegate represents that country’s position on the topics being discussed. Arguments should reflect the assigned country’s foreign policy, interests and approach to the issue rather than personal beliefs.

For example, a country whose economy relies heavily on fossil fuels may take a different position on climate change from a country that prioritises renewable energy. A delegate’s responsibilities include:

  • Researching the assigned country’s political positions and policies.
  • Understanding the topics on the committee’s agenda.
  • Preparing arguments and possible solutions.
  • Participating in formal and informal debates.
  • Collaborating with other delegates to develop resolutions.
  • Representing the country professionally throughout the conference.

Disagreement is an important part of diplomacy. What matters is communicating, negotiating and working towards a possible solution.

2. Before the conference: preparation and research

Good preparation can make a difference to the MUN experience. Public speaking matters, but understanding the topic and the country’s position helps delegates participate meaningfully. Begin by learning about the country’s government, foreign policy, international relationships and previous involvement in the issue. Consider what it supports, opposes and hopes to achieve.

Research the topic from different perspectives. Learn about its causes and consequences, relevant international agreements, previous UN actions and possible solutions. Familiarity with the subject makes it easier to contribute to discussion.

3. Understanding the structure of a MUN committee

A committee session is led by the dais, which oversees proceedings and makes sure debate follows the established rules of procedure. The dais usually includes a Chairperson, who leads sessions, recognises speakers and manages debate; a Vice-Chairperson, who assists and may take over certain responsibilities; and a Rapporteur, who handles administrative duties, attendance, documentation and records, depending on the conference structure.

Delegates address the Chair rather than speaking over one another. The Chairperson maintains order and gives everyone a fair opportunity to participate.

4. How does a MUN session begin?

The Chairperson officially opens proceedings. During roll call, the Chair calls each country to establish attendance. Delegates typically respond in one of two ways:

  • Present: the delegate is attending and may abstain during substantive voting.
  • Present and Voting: the delegate is attending and will vote in favour or against substantive matters; abstentions are not permitted.

The response determines how the delegate may vote later. If the committee has more than one topic, delegates may establish which topic will be discussed first by making a motion to set the agenda.

5. Understanding the different types of debate

Debate lets delegates present their country’s position, challenge arguments, exchange ideas and develop possible solutions.

Speakers’ List. This is the primary form of formal debate. Delegates raise their placards to be added to the list, and the Chair recognises them one at a time. A typical opening speech introduces the topic, explains the country’s position and concerns, and offers possible solutions.

Moderated caucus. This is a focused debate on a specific part of the agenda. Speeches are shorter, and the Chair recognises delegates throughout. A proposal states the topic, total duration and speaking time.

Unmoderated caucus. This is an informal period when delegates may move around, communicate, negotiate and collaborate. They can find countries with similar positions, form working groups, discuss solutions, negotiate disagreements and begin drafting resolutions. It is often where much of the practical work happens.

6. Motions and points

A motion is a formal proposal asking the committee to take an action. Common motions include:

  • Motion to Open Debate.
  • Motion to Set the Agenda.
  • Motion for a Moderated Caucus.
  • Motion for an Unmoderated Caucus.
  • Motion to Introduce a Draft Resolution.
  • Motion to Close Debate.

A motion generally requires the Chair’s recognition and may need to be seconded or voted on, depending on the rules of procedure.

A point is a procedural request or question. Common points include a Point of Personal Privilege when something prevents a delegate from participating comfortably or hearing a speaker; a Point of Order when a delegate believes procedure is not being followed; and a Point of Parliamentary Inquiry to ask the Chair about procedure. A motion proposes an action; a point raises a procedural concern or question.

7. How to make an effective speech

An effective MUN speech is clear, structured, persuasive and relevant to the country’s position. A useful structure is: opening, explanation, evidence, solutions and closing. Introduce the country’s position, explain why the issue matters, support the argument with relevant facts or examples, offer practical recommendations and end with a clear statement.

Diplomatic language helps maintain a respectful atmosphere. Delegates might say “The delegation of [country] believes that…”, “This delegation would like to emphasise…”, “We encourage the committee to consider…” or “The delegation respectfully disagrees with…”.

8. Negotiation, alliances and working papers

After delegates discuss the issue and identify different perspectives, the committee works towards possible solutions. Delegates with similar ideas may form blocs or working groups, discuss priorities, exchange proposals and develop solutions together.

A working paper is an informal document outlining a group’s ideas and proposed solutions. It provides a starting point for a more formal resolution and is generally submitted to the dais for review according to conference rules.

9. Draft resolutions

A draft resolution is a formal document containing the committee’s proposed solutions. It shows the committee moving beyond discussion to practical recommendations. A resolution generally has two sections:

Preambulatory clauses introduce the issue, provide context and refer to previous international agreements, concerns or actions. They explain why the committee believes action is necessary. Common phrases include “Recognising”, “Concerned by”, “Acknowledging”, “Recalling”, “Emphasising” and “Noting with concern”.

Operative clauses outline actions and solutions proposed by the committee. Common phrases include “Calls upon”, “Encourages”, “Recommends”, “Requests”, “Supports”, “Urges” and “Establishes”. Clauses should be specific and realistic. After a draft resolution is submitted and approved for introduction, it can be presented for discussion.

10. Amendments

An amendment is a proposed change to an existing draft resolution. It may add a new operative clause, remove a clause, change its wording or modify a proposed solution. Amendments allow delegates to improve a resolution and address concerns. Depending on conference rules, amendments may be friendly, meaning relevant sponsors accept them, or unfriendly, meaning further consideration and a committee vote are required.

11. Voting procedure

When debate is ready to close, delegates move to voting on a proposed draft resolution. Delegates generally vote in favour, against or abstain. Abstentions may not be available to delegates who declared themselves Present and Voting during roll call. The required majority depends on the conference’s rules and the type of motion or resolution. The Chair announces the result after voting.

If a draft resolution receives the required votes, the committee adopts it. MUN resolutions are generally recommendations produced through an academic simulation; they do not automatically become legally binding United Nations resolutions.
